The first-order model fits each individual step closely (RMSE ≤ 3.7°), but one set of parameters cannot describe the motor over the whole PWM range: U/ω is not constant (≈ 0.3 V·s/rad near ±10 %, rising to ≈ 0.75 V·s/rad at ±100 %), and T_m falls from ≈ 0.35–0.7 s at low PWM to ≈ 0.07 s at full power. With a single k_e per wheel (0.70 / 0.68 V·s/rad), the model under-predicts the wheel’s travel by about 50 % at ±10 % PWM and 25–30 % at ±30 %, and is within 10 % only above ±60 %; the dead zone is smaller than our 5 % step (the wheels stayed still at 0 % but turned at ±5 %), so we could only bound it to |U| < 0.56 V. Driving forward, the two wheels are almost identical (within ±4 % from 15 % to 100 % PWM, mean +0.5 %), but in reverse the right wheel is faster at every level from −15 % to −100 % (mean +8 %, up to +14 %) and it also responds more slowly (median T_m 0.26 s vs 0.20 s). Next week’s odometry integrates the encoder readings, so it will still follow what the wheels really do; the mismatch matters wherever we predict motion from the commanded PWM, as in the simulator, open-loop driving and go-to-goal. With equal PWM on both wheels the robot drives an arc of radius R ≈ b/ε (b = track width, ε = relative speed difference): almost straight forward (R ≈ 200 b), but a clear curve in reverse (R ≈ 12 b, about 2.5 m for b = 0.2 m). The different time constants will also turn the robot a little every time it starts or stops, so the PWM-based heading will drift away from the encoder odometry, and each wheel needs its own parameters (or closed-loop speed control) to drive straight.
